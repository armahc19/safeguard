SafeGuard reaches that category by turning FIR and PDR into one fraud graph, then selling two uses of it: an investigator can walk the money, and a partner can screen a transfer. The work is a sequence of product decisions and data, not a new stack.

You already have the raw materials. FIR holds reports, incidents, roles, and findings. PDR holds identities, wallets, and transfers. A projected graph already joins them with owns, transferred_to, and same_phone_reference, and it scores an entity from nearby reports. What you do not have yet is the thing Chainalysis, TRM, and Elliptic charge for: exposure along a path, with a crime type and an evidence tier.

The sequence

1. Write the graph contract.
Decide the only nodes and edges the company believes in. Nodes: person, phone, wallet, account, agent, merchant, partner, incident. Edges from PDR: owns, associated phone, held at, transferred to. Edges from FIR: reported, victim of, suspect in, same incident. Joins: a verified national ID may resolve to the same person; a shared phone is only an association. Every edge carries source, time, confidence, and an evidence tier: allegation, corroborated, partner-confirmed, investigator-confirmed. Individual transfers stay as events. An aggregated transferred_to is a view of those events, because exposure needs amount and time, not just "these two wallets have met."

Done when a reviewer can point at any wallet and see which facts came from FIR, which came from PDR, and which are still candidates.

2. Define exposure.
This is the product sentence: "In the last 7 days, 40% of this wallet's inflows sit 2 hops from a confirmed impersonation incident, through one collection wallet and one cash-out agent." Split it into direct exposure (the counterparty itself) and indirect exposure (hops). Cap the hops. Weight by amount and recency. Exclude victim-role wallets from the "bad" side, or you will flag people who paid a scammer. Absence of a transfer means the partner did not send it, not that the wallet is clean.

Done when the same wallet always produces the same path, the same percentage, and the same typology, and a human can audit the path.

3. Publish a small typology library.
Start with the Ghana mobile-money patterns you can already see in FIR cases and the benchmark files. A useful first set is impersonation with a cash-out, fake reversal, fan-in to one collection wallet, pass-through mule, agent hotspot, and SIM-swap then drain. Each typology is a graph shape plus the FIR label that confirms it. Keywords stay in Shield. They do not become intelligence by themselves.

Done when each typology has a name, a version, the hops it needs, and the evidence tier required before it can drive a block.

4. Add behavioral clusters as candidates.
Exact ID match will not find the rings. Group wallets that fan in, fan out, pass through, or cash out at the same agent, and mark every group as a candidate. An investigator, or a second independent signal, promotes the candidate into a FIR incident. That promotion is what raises exposure for everyone who paid the cluster. A shared phone never becomes "same person" at this step.

Done when a candidate cluster can be opened, rejected, or confirmed, and a rejection does not leave a permanent taint.

5. Ship Investigate on top of that graph.
Start from a FIR report or a PDR wallet. Expand hop by hop. Show roles, amounts, typologies, and evidence tiers. Let the analyst confirm a cluster, assign victim or suspect, and write a finding. The finding writes back to FIR and changes exposure. This is Reactor, Discovery, and TRM Forensics.

Done when an analyst can finish a case without leaving the graph, and the case leaves a label behind.

6. Ship Screen once exposure is trustworthy.
A partner submits a sender, a receiver, an amount, and a time. SafeGuard returns category, direct exposure, indirect exposure, hop count, evidence tier, and one action: allow, review, or block, plus the path in words. The partner never receives the other institution's raw rows. This is KYT, Lens, and TRM's screening API. It is the reason the next partner sends PDR.

Done when a partner can block or step up a transfer from the response alone, and a later dispute can reconstruct why.

7. Make coverage the business.
One partner's ledger is that partner's own fraud system. The company starts when both sides of a transfer are in the graph. Run this in parallel with steps 1–6, or the graph stays empty.

• Sign telcos and wallets for identities, accounts, transfers, and cash-out agent IDs. Agent and merchant IDs matter more here than they do on a blockchain.
• Agree latency: batch is enough for investigations, screening needs the transfer before it settles.
• Agree the return: risk, typology, and action. Not another bank's customers.
• Measure one number: the share of transfers where both endpoints are known.

8. Calibrate against real outcomes.
Use reviewer decisions, partner confirmations, and the Ghana MoMo benchmark cases as ground truth. Track three things: did Screen catch confirmed fraud, did it flag victims, and did a bad cluster merge spread taint. A typology that fails gets a new version. It does not silently change old decisions.

What has to run beside the product

• Privacy and Bank of Ghana rules, before a second partner connects. Protected references stay the join keys. Cross-institution answers stay inside SafeGuard.
• An evidence policy. One user report can justify "review." Only corroborated or confirmed evidence can justify "block."
• An analyst playbook. Who promotes a cluster, who can mark a victim, and how a false positive is undone.

What to leave until the graph is real

Law-enforcement exports, sanctions-style lists, and a large machine-learning scorer. A model on top of a thin graph imitates those companies. It does not become them. Their moat is labeled flow across institutions.

Build steps 1 and 2 first, and start partner coverage the same week. Typologies and clusters make the graph smart. Investigate makes it trustworthy. Screen is what you sell. I would not open Screen to a partner until exposure can show a path and can tell a victim from a mule.

Chainalysis, TRM Labs, and Elliptic do not start from the fraud case. They start from the transfer. The case only hangs a label on wallets and transfers that the ledger already created. FIR and PDR are already the two ingredients. The graph is the order you connect them.

How they build it

A transaction creates the wallets. On a blockchain every payment is public: address A sent this amount to address B at this time. The first time an address appears, it becomes a node. The payment becomes an arrow from A to B. Nobody registers a wallet first. The transfer brings both wallets into the graph.

A wallet is a pile of addresses they later decide are one actor. One person uses many addresses. They group addresses that spend together, or that sweep into the same exchange account, into one wallet. Until that grouping, the graph is just addresses and arrows.

A name is added on top. "This wallet is Binance." "This wallet is a scam." That name comes from a customer, a victim report, a seizure, or an investigator. The name does not draw a new money arrow. It sticks to a wallet that already has arrows.

A fraud case points at existing pieces. A victim says, "I paid this address." They find that payment in the ledger, mark the sender as the victim, mark the receiver as the suspect, and mark that payment as the loss. Then they follow the arrows: where did the money go next, and where did it cash out? The case file is the story. The graph is the wallets and the arrows. The case holds links to both.

Exposure is just walking the arrows. If a wallet is labeled scam, every wallet that paid it is one hop away. Wallets that paid those are two hops away. That walk is the product.

The same build for mobile money

PDR is the ledger. FIR is the label. Build the graph in that order.

1. Let each PDR transfer create the money graph. When a transfer arrives, find or create the sender wallet and the receiver wallet, then draw one arrow: this wallet sent this amount to that wallet at this time. A wallet node is born the first time it appears in a transfer. Do this for every successful transfer. Those arrows are the Chainalysis graph.

2. Let PDR identity hang the owner on the wallet. When a partner says which person holds a wallet, and which phone is on that person, add the person and the phone and connect them to the wallet that step 1 already created. Mobile money is ahead of crypto here, because the partner already knows the registered owner. Crypto companies have to guess.

3. Join the same person across partners. The same verified national ID at two partners becomes one person. The same phone is only a link between two records, not proof it is one person. This is their "many addresses, one wallet" step, using KYC instead of spending patterns.

4. Let a FIR case point at nodes that already exist. A report mentions a phone, a wallet, or a payment. Look those up in the graph. Create the case and connect three things: the victim, the suspect, and the exact transfer that was the loss. If that phone or wallet has never appeared in PDR, keep it as a reported node with no money arrow. The arrow appears later, when a partner sends the transfer.

5. Follow the money the way they do. From the suspect wallet, walk the transfer arrows forward to the next wallet and the cash-out. Walk backward to see who else paid that wallet. The other payers are the rest of the ring. That walk is the investigation.

6. Put the label back on the wallet. When the case is only a user report, the wallet is alleged. When a reviewer confirms it, the wallet is confirmed fraud. From then on, any new transfer into that wallet is one hop from a confirmed case. That is exposure, and it is what a partner would screen.

A cash-out agent is the mobile-money version of an exchange. If the receiver of a transfer is an agent, that agent is a node too, and many scam wallets paying one agent is the end of the trail.

You already have FIR and PDR. The move is to treat PDR transfers as the only thing that creates wallets and money arrows, and to treat each FIR case as a set of pointers onto those wallets and arrows.    Once that is true, opening a case and walking two hops is the Chainalysis idea.

Let's build NEXUS around Case-First Intelligence.

I actually like this direction for SafeGuard because you already have FIR and PDR implemented. We can make the fraud investigation the starting point, then use partner financial data to expand the investigation.

But there's an important distinction:

FIR creates the investigation. PDR supplies the financial evidence. NEXUS connects everything and reveals the bigger picture.

We are not designing an autonomous AI investigator yet. We're designing the intelligence infrastructure that investigators and, eventually, AI agents can use.
1. The Case-First NEXUS philosophy
Imagine somebody reports a MoMo scam.

They provide:
- Their phone number.
- Suspected scammer's phone number.
- Receiving MoMo account.
- Amount lost.
- Transaction reference.
- Scam message or other evidence.
The moment the FIR is created, NEXUS should have something to investigate.

It shouldn't wait for MTN or Telecel to provide transaction data before creating the investigation.
FIR — Investigation Origin

A victim reports a suspected fraud incident.


You don't need to build another FIR or PDR. You need to turn the data already inside FIR and PDR into a financial intelligence graph.

That's the actual Chainalysis idea.
1. First, understand the architecture
Look at the responsibilities:
PDR — Financial Activity

Transactions, accounts, wallets, providers, ownership and identity records  


- Every previously unknown account creates an account node.
- Every transaction creates a directed relationship.
- Every transaction has its own amount, timestamp, reference and provider.
- Existing accounts are reused rather than duplicated.
Notice something interesting: two different people paid the same receiving wallet.

That is potentially interesting intelligence.

But it isn't automatically fraud.
3. The real power: Connecting FIR to the graph
Suppose someone submits an FIR:
I was deceived by someone using 0241111111. I transferred GH₵500 to 0552222222.

NEXUS should:
1. Find the reported phone number.
2. Find its associated identity and wallet records.
3. Locate the GH₵500 transaction in PDR.
4. Link the victim, suspect, wallet and transaction to the FIR.
5. Display the surrounding transaction network.
6. Preserve the distinction between allegations and verified findings.
Now the investigator isn't just reading a report.

They're investigating a financial network.
4. What I think NEXUS should actually become
I would organize the intelligence into five capabilities.
Capability	What it does
Entity Intelligence	Understands people, phones, wallets, accounts and businesses
Transaction Intelligence	Understands who paid whom, when and how much
Fraud Intelligence	Connects reported and confirmed fraud entities
Network Intelligence	Reveals connected accounts, suspicious clusters and money trails
Exposure Intelligence	Identifies entities connected to known fraudulent entities

This is the foundation of a serious financial intelligence platform.
5. But there is one important difference from crypto
Chainalysis can inspect a public blockchain ledger.

SafeGuard cannot automatically see every MTN, Telecel, bank or fintech transaction.

Your graph is only as complete as the partner data you receive.

Also, don't assume the same phone number means the same person, or that two accounts sharing an identity are necessarily controlled by the same person. You need identity confidence, provenance and verification.
